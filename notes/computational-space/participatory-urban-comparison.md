# Compare urban options without hiding disagreement

A proposed comparison session that preserves individual priorities and makes the basis of urban design choices visible. No participant study or preference outcome is claimed.

## Question

When several urban proposals are shown together, which differences help participants reason, and which simply reward the most polished rendering? This exercise asks how to compare access, outdoor space, building distribution, and perceived comfort without collapsing disagreement into one score. The intended unit of discussion is a specific tradeoff linked to a place in the proposal. The question concerns the design of a comparison process, not evidence that any option is preferred.

## Design proposal

Propose three synchronized views using identical camera positions, lighting, scale, and annotation styles. Pair them with a small criteria table whose rows explain how each quantity was obtained. Let participants add a priority and attach a comment to a location. Keep personal rankings separate until discussion ends. The shared record should distinguish measured geometry, participant interpretation, and unresolved questions, so a persuasive comment cannot quietly become a measured fact.

## Procedure

Prepare three variants from the same hypothetical site and retain a written account of what changed. Start the session with an individual inspection and a short ranking task. Then invite participants to explain one advantage and one concern for each option. Record comments against stable feature identifiers. Revisit rankings after discussion, preserving the earlier versions. Close by listing missing information that could change a decision rather than forcing the group to select a winner.

## Evaluation

Evaluate whether participants can identify the intended design differences and trace a criterion to its measurement. Compare initial and final explanations, recording changes in reasoning without interpreting every change as improvement. Count comments that cannot be attached to a concrete feature and criteria whose definitions generate confusion. Check that dissent remains visible in the session record. Any participant recruitment, sample size, and analysis plan should be decided before collecting study data.

## Limitations and next steps

A small discussion cannot represent a neighborhood, and a synchronized rendering still omits lived conditions such as seasonal use and maintenance. Participants may also avoid disagreement when a designer is present. The next step is to test the materials in a low stakes rehearsal, revise confusing criteria, and identify whose perspective is absent. Later studies should document recruitment, consent, and facilitation choices before making claims about participatory value or community preference.
