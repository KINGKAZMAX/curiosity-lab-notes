# Present multiple interpretations without inventing a consensus

A proposed exhibit writing exercise that keeps interpretations attributable and comparable. It uses newly drafted fictional exhibit records and does not invent community testimony or interviews.

## Question

How can a digital exhibit show that an object may be interpreted differently without suggesting that all accounts are interchangeable? This exercise examines the relationship between evidence, interpretation, and the person or institution making a claim. It asks whether visitors can identify whose perspective they are reading and why two accounts differ. The goal is a legible structure for disagreement, not a fabricated balance of opinions or a claim that a community shares one view.

## Design proposal

Propose a shared object record followed by separately attributed interpretation panels. Each panel should state its source, scope, and relationship to the available evidence. Place factual disagreements beside the affected claims rather than burying them in a general note. Include community perspectives only when appropriate source material and permission are available. A missing perspective should appear as a documented gap or question, never as a newly invented quotation that sounds representative.

## Procedure

Choose one publicly documented item and collect two existing descriptions that can be appropriately referenced. Separate their observations from their interpretations without rewriting either as an opposing position for dramatic effect. Draft a neutral orientation paragraph identifying what both accounts discuss. Add precise source links to each panel. Have a reviewer mark unattributed statements, assumed group identities, and places where the interface makes one interpretation seem final through visual prominence alone.

## Evaluation

Evaluate whether readers can attribute each account, distinguish an observation from an interpretation, and explain the specific point of disagreement. Inspect whether the layout preserves important differences in source quality rather than presenting equal visual space as equal evidential weight. Record misunderstandings and requests for missing context. Do not treat agreement with the curator as success. The relevant outcome is the reader's ability to navigate the documented accounts and their limits.
